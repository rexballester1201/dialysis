Why it stopped you

This system refuses things. That is deliberate — most of its rules exist because the alternative harms someone. Every refusal here has a reason and a way forward. Find what you saw on screen, and it will tell you both.

Starting a treatment

Most first-thing-in-the-morning refusals come from one of two checks: the water, and the machine.

“No water check has been logged for today.” I can’t start anyone.

Blocked

The first treatment of the day cannot begin until someone has measured total chlorine and recorded it. Chlorine and chloramine pass straight through an RO membrane; when the carbon beds are exhausted they reach the dialysate, and across a dialyzer membrane that haemolyses the patient.

No reading is not a pass. An unmeasured carbon bed is not a safe one, so silence fails closed rather than open.

Do this

Have the technician run the test strip and record the result. Once a passing check is on file, the day opens — and the check is not asked for again before every patient, only before the first.

“Total chlorine is 0.15 ppm, above the 0.1 ppm action limit.”

Blocked

The water is out of specification. 0.1 ppm total chlorine is the AAMI/ISO 23500 action level, and it is the figure written into this system’s schema. Above it, nobody dialyses.

Do this

Correct the carbon beds, then log a fresh check. The day opens on the new passing reading, not by overriding the old one — there is no override for this.

“Machine M-0042 is under_repair and cannot be used.”

Blocked

A machine marked under_repair, quarantined or retired is unavailable regardless of what the board says. If it failed a safety test, the system took it out of service at the moment the failure was recorded.

Do this

Use another machine. To bring this one back, biomed records the corrective maintenance and returns it to service.

“Record the pre-dialysis weight before starting.”

Blocked

The UF goal is calculated from the pre-dialysis weight against the dry weight. Starting without it produces a fluid-removal target derived from nothing.

Do this

Weigh the patient and complete check-in, then start.

“A session that is scheduled cannot become in_progress.”

By design

The treatment runs in order: scheduled → checked in → in progress → completed. You have tried to skip check-in. The columns only make sense in sequence — an end time on a session that never started produces a duration that is quietly wrong rather than obviously missing.

Chairs and machines

Infection-control segregation is the highest-consequence rule in the unit. It is the only rule in the system that both refuses and yields to a reason.

“Station S-01 is not designated for the HBV cohort.”

Blocked

The patient’s infection-control cohort — clean, hbv or hcv — is not set by hand. It is derived from their most recent result for each serology marker, so filing one HBsAg result can move a patient between cohorts and change which chairs they may use, including for bookings already on tomorrow’s board.

Do this

Move the patient to a chair their cohort permits. If none is available and the treatment cannot wait, see the override below.

“That machine last treated a HBV patient and has had no disinfection cycle since.”

Blocked

A machine carries the previous patient’s blood path until a cycle is recorded. This is a categorical rule, not an interval one — how often a machine must otherwise be cleaned is your unit’s policy and the system does not assert a number, but that it must be cleaned between cohorts is not negotiable.

Do this

Run the disinfection cycle and record it. The machine clears immediately.

Our only HBV chair is broken and the patient needs dialysing today.

By design

Infection-control segregation accepts an override, because a rule with no override gets worked around outside the system — where nothing records it at all. Supply a reason of at least ten characters and the assignment proceeds.

The override is written onto the chart as an INFECTION CONTROL OVERRIDE note where a reviewing clinician will see it, it lands in the audit trail, and the response says plainly what was overridden so no screen can succeed quietly.

Note

“ok” will be rejected. Write a reason that still means something to whoever reads this record in a year.

“Machine M-0042 has run 3 treatments since it was last disinfected.”

Warned

This one does not stop you. It is a statement of fact from the data, not a verdict: the system does not know your cleaning interval and will not invent one. In a unit that disinfects between every patient, any number above zero is worth acting on.

“Dialyzer DZ-0041 is at 74.5% of its original total cell volume.”

Blocked

Below 80% of new, the fibre bundle has clotted off far enough that clearance is no longer what was prescribed — the patient would be dialysed less than the chart claims. The unit is refused at the point of issue and condemned at its next reprocessing.

The same check refuses a dialyzer that has reached its reuse count, and one that belongs to a different patient — that last one is a cross-infection event, not a paperwork slip.

Charting

The flow sheet is append-only. Nothing you write is edited in place, which is what makes it safe to chart from two devices at once.

“An observation for this session at that time is already recorded.”

By design

Vitals are keyed by session and the exact time recorded. A second reading at the same instant is a duplicate, not a new observation — which is precisely what makes the flow sheet conflict-free between a tablet that has been offline and a desk that has not. Two devices charting the same session cannot produce a merge conflict, only a duplicate the system absorbs.

Do this

If the first reading was wrong, add a corrected one at the actual time it was taken. Nothing is overwritten and both stay on the record.

“Heparin is a high-alert medication and requires a witness.”

Blocked

High-alert drugs need an independent second check. The system names the drug rather than handing you a constraint error, so you know which one it means.

“The nurse giving it cannot witness their own dose.”

Blocked

A witness who is the same person is not a second check, which is the entire point of the rule. The database only requires the field to be filled; the system is deliberately stricter than that.

“hypotensionn” was rejected as an event code.

Blocked

Event codes come from a fixed list. A typo would otherwise create a category that no report ever counts, so the unrecognised code is refused rather than quietly stored.

I entered a Kt/V of 1.9 and it saved 1.32.

By design

Kt/V and URR are derived, and the server derives them from the BUN samples rather than storing what it was sent. A chart holding a Kt/V that disagrees with its own blood results is a chart that will be believed and is wrong — and Kt/V is what decides whether a prescription gets lengthened.

One exception: a Kt/V from the machine’s own online-clearance or ionic sensor is a measurement, not a derivation, and is kept exactly as recorded. Recomputing over it would replace a real reading with an estimate.

Signing and changing

A treatment record locks when both signatures are in. After that there is one way in, and it leaves a trail.

“Cannot sign, missing: post_weight_kg, ended_at.”

Blocked

A record cannot be signed with its clinically mandatory fields empty: pre and post weight, start and end times, and a named primary nurse. Signing attests that the treatment happened as recorded, and an incomplete record cannot be attested to.

“Session was signed and locked. Corrections go through the amendment path.”

Blocked

Both signatures are in and the record is closed. This is not a permissions problem — you have the right role, the record has simply moved on. If your tablet was offline when it locked, your observation was not discarded: it was reported back to you as a conflict.

Do this

A nephrologist amends the record with a reason. The original values stay put; the correction becomes a session note and a full before/after pair in the audit log. The record stays locked.

Why do two people have to sign?

By design

The nurse attests to what was delivered; the nephrologist countersigns it. Neither signature alone locks the record, and a nurse cannot countersign as the nephrologist. Once both are in, the treatment record becomes the legal account of what happened.

Why does the amendment ask me to write a reason?

By design

An amendment without a reason is indistinguishable from someone quietly fixing a number. The reason becomes part of the chart, so write one that will still make sense to a reader in a year.

Billing

A claim is what the payer is asked for; an invoice is what is left. Neither rate nor cap is written into the software.

“Session … is already on claim CLM-202608-0003.”

Blocked

A session is billed at most once, and the system tells you which claim already has it rather than just refusing. If the whole batch is rejected for one duplicate, that is intentional — a claim covering four of six sessions is worse than none, because someone then has to work out which two are missing.

“Session … is not signed. Only a locked record may be claimed.”

Blocked

An unsigned record is work nobody has attested to yet. Billing it puts a claim in front of a payer for a treatment the unit has not finished certifying.

“Only 1 of the 156 allotted remain in the period ending 2026-12-31.”

Blocked

The annual allotment is nearly spent. Note that 156 is not written into the software — it is read from the benefit programme in force on the service date. The PhilHealth case rate has moved ₱2,600 → ₱4,000 → ₱6,350 and the allotment 90 → 156; superseded programmes stay on file so a historical claim re-prices against what was in force at the time.

The invoice says the patient owes nothing. Is that right?

By design

If the benefit programme forbids balance billing, it is right. Charging the patient the difference is exactly what that rule prohibits, so the system sets their share to zero whatever the list price came to.

“A claim approved at zero is a denial, and a denial needs a code.”

Blocked

A denial with no code is a claim nobody can resubmit. Record what the payer’s advice actually said — the claim’s status then follows the money rather than being asserted alongside it, so a claim cannot read “paid” while its remittance says otherwise.

Offline and tablets

The bedside tablet is built to lose the network and keep working. This is the part of the system the unit depends on most.

The tablet had no signal all shift. Did I lose my charting?

By design

No. Everything you chart is written to a local outbox first and sent when the network returns. The outbox is the authoritative record on the device — the cached patient list is only a read-only copy.

My session expired while I was offline. Will it drop my queue?

By design

No. An expired token is never treated as “these operations failed”. The queue is kept, you re-authenticate with your PIN, and the same batch replays.

The tablet resent everything. Did it double up my readings?

By design

No. Every operation carries an identifier minted on the device, and a replayed batch returns the stored answer without touching a single clinical row. If one row in a batch is malformed, only that row is rejected — a nurse never loses four hours of charting to one bad payload.

Why a 6-digit PIN instead of my password?

By design

Nurses re-authenticate roughly twenty times a shift. Demanding a full password that often guarantees one shared logged-in account, which destroys the audit trail — so the PIN is a deliberate trade. It is fenced in: the tablet must already have completed a full password login for you, the route is rate-limited, and every attempt is recorded.

I got signed out on a different tablet and my session was killed.

By design

Each login is bound to the device it was issued on. A credential presented from a second tablet has been copied, so it is revoked rather than refused — refusing it would leave a working credential in the wrong hands. Log in again on the device you are actually holding.

Patient records

Nothing clinical is ever really deleted, and the things that change over time are kept as history rather than overwritten.

“The birth date cannot be in the future.”

Blocked

Usually a typo in the year. Worth knowing: the database itself cannot enforce this one, so if it were not checked at entry it would not be checked anywhere.

I changed the status on the patient form and it did not save.

By design

Status changes have their own step, so a history entry can never be skipped. “When did this patient transfer out?” is a question the chart has to answer years later, and the status field alone cannot answer it.

I set a new dry weight and last month’s IDWG figures did not move.

By design

Correct. A dry weight is effective-dated, not a field you overwrite, and each session records the target that was in force on the day it ran. If the new weight rewrote history, every past session’s fluid numbers would silently change meaning.

I deleted a patient but they are still in the database.

By design

Deletion is a soft delete and only an administrator can do it. Sessions, claims and the audit trail all reference the patient; a chart that vanishes cannot be audited.

Does the system record that I opened a chart?

By design

Yes. Every chart view is logged — who opened whose record, and when. That is a different question from who changed what, and it is the one privacy regulators actually ask. Opening a treatment record counts as opening that patient’s chart.

Still stuck?

Two rules of thumb. If the message names a chair, machine, dialyzer or the water, it is an infection-control or equipment rule and the fix is physical — move, clean, or replace. If it names a signature, a reason or a claim, it is a record-keeping rule and the fix is a step you have not taken yet.

If a refusal looks wrong to you, it is worth reporting rather than working around. The rules in this system were each put there because the alternative harmed someone — but a rule firing in a situation nobody anticipated is still a bug.

A note on this document. The screens described here are not built yet — the bedside and console applications are scaffolds. What is complete and tested is the system underneath: the rules, the refusals and the records. Any interface built on it will enforce exactly what is written above.

Clinical thresholds cited: total chlorine 0.1 ppm (AAMI/ISO 23500), Kt/V ≥ 1.2 and URR ≥ 65% (KDOQI 2015), dialyzer TCV floor 80% of new. Verify each against your unit’s own standard operating procedures before go-live.
