The dialysis day

One unit, four shifts, one record per treatment. This is what the system expects of you at each point in the day, and — just as usefully — where it will stop you, and why.

The shape of the day

Patients hold a standing pattern — a set of weekdays and a shift, such as Mon/Wed/Fri on AM. The board for any date is generated from those patterns, so nobody rebuilds the roster by hand each morning.

AM · MORNING 06:00 – 10:30 Mon – Sat
MID · MIDDAY 10:45 – 15:15 Mon – Sat
PM · AFTERNOON 15:30 – 20:00 Mon – Sat
NOC · NIGHT 20:15 – 00:45 Mon / Wed / Fri
Turnaround

The 15-minute gaps between shifts are the unit's turnaround window. A chair holds one patient per shift per day, and the system will not let a second one be booked into it — that refusal is the fifteen minutes protecting itself.

Who does what

Your role decides what you can reach. These are not seniority tiers — a technician can do things a nurse cannot, because the machines and the water are their job.

RoleWhat it is for
nephrologistPrescribes. Countersigns treatment records. The only role that can amend a locked one.
head_nurseRoster, scheduling, clinical oversight. Builds the day's board.
nurseRuns sessions and charts the flow sheet. Signs as the delivering nurse.
technicianMachines, water testing, dialyzer reprocessing — their own permissions, not borrowed from nursing.
billingClaims, invoices, payer follow-up. No clinical write access.
recordsRegistration and documents.
dietitianNutrition assessment. Nothing is built for it yet, so the role can read and cannot write.
adminUnit settings and roles, registration, scheduling, the water log and billing. Cannot chart, sign or prescribe. The only role that can retire a patient record.
readonlyAudit and management view. Reads the clinical record and the unit's reports, writes nothing. Claims and invoices stay with billing.
On the tablet

Sign in once with your password on the device you will use. After that a 6-digit PIN unlocks it for the rest of the shift. Your login is bound to that tablet: if the same credential appears on another device it is revoked rather than refused, because a credential in two places has been copied.

The treatment run

These eight steps are a real sequence — the record cannot skip one, and the numbers are the order the system enforces rather than a reading convenience. A square marks each check it makes: red stops you, amber tells you something, teal is what gets recorded.

0Technician
Before 06:00

Test the water

Measure total chlorine and record it. No treatment in the day can start until this exists — not as paperwork, but because chlorine passes straight through an RO membrane and haemolyses across a dialyzer.

  • Above 0.1 ppm total chlorine, the day does not open. Correct the carbon beds and log a fresh check. There is no override for this one.
  • No check at all is treated as a failure, not as an unknown. An unmeasured carbon bed is not a safe one.
  • Conductivity, pH and the rest are recorded alongside for the trend. Only chlorine gates the day.
1Head nurse
Start of shift

Generate the board

The day's chairs are built from the standing patterns. Each patient arrives on the board carrying their infection-control cohort; their prescription and the dry weight in force that day are attached when they are checked in.

  • One chair, one patient, per shift, per day. A double-booking is refused at the point of scheduling, not discovered at the bedside.
  • The board is built only from standing patterns. An add-on or a reschedule has no screen yet, so it goes through the administrator until one exists.
2Nurse
On arrival

Check in

Weigh the patient, then assign the chair and the machine. This is where infection control is decided, so it is where most refusals happen.

  • The chair must suit the cohort. An HBV-positive patient cannot take a clean station.
  • The machine must have been disinfected since its last patient of a different cohort. Record the cycle and it clears at once.
  • You are told how many runs the machine has done since it was last cleaned. That one does not stop you — your unit sets the interval, not the software.
  • The pre-dialysis weight sets the UF goal against the dry weight in force today.
If there is genuinely no compliant chair

Infection control accepts an override with a written reason of at least ten characters, because a rule with no way through gets worked around outside the system, where nothing records it at all. The override is written onto the chart as an INFECTION CONTROL OVERRIDE note and into the audit trail. Write a reason that will still mean something in a year.

3Nurse
On the needle

Start

Starting stamps the time the treatment actually began. Everything downstream — duration, UF rate, adequacy — is measured from it, so it is recorded as it happens rather than estimated afterwards.

  • The day's water check must be on file and passing.
  • The machine must be in service. One marked under repair, quarantined or retired is unavailable whatever the board says.
  • The pre-dialysis weight must be recorded. Without it the UF goal is derived from nothing.
  • The dialyzer must be this patient's own, above 80% of its original total cell volume, and within its reuse count. It is checked when its label is entered at the start.
  • Draw the pre-dialysis BUN now if adequacy is being measured this session. The post sample at step 5 is useless on its own.
4Nurse
Every 30 min

Chart the run

Observations and events go onto the flow sheet as they happen; medication doses will too, once their screen exists. The flow sheet is append-only — nothing is edited in place, which is what lets a tablet that has been offline and a desk that has not both chart the same session without ever colliding.

  • Each observation is keyed to the time it was taken. A correction is a new entry at the real time, not an edit, and both stay on the record.
  • High-alert medications need a witness, and the witness cannot be the person giving the dose. A second check by the same person is not a second check.
  • Event codes come from a fixed list. A typo would create a category no report ever counts, so it is refused rather than quietly stored.
  • A dose not given is recorded as not given, with a reason. A blank is not the same fact.
5Nurse
Off the needle

End

Record the end time, the post-dialysis weight, and the post BUN if adequacy is being measured. The system then does the arithmetic itself.

  • Kt/V and URR are calculated by the server from the BUN samples. The tablet never sends a figure of its own, and when both samples are recorded any figure sent from elsewhere is replaced — a chart holding a Kt/V that disagrees with its own blood results is one that will be believed and is wrong.
  • A Kt/V from the machine's own online-clearance sensor is a measurement, not an estimate, and is kept exactly as recorded.
  • Interdialytic weight gain, fluid removed and actual duration are derived from what you entered, never typed in.
  • A UF rate above roughly 13 ml/kg/h is flagged for review. It is a marker, not a verdict — the clinical call is yours.
6Nurse, then
nephrologist

Sign

The nurse attests to what was delivered; the nephrologist countersigns. When both signatures are in, the record locks and becomes the account of what happened.

  • An incomplete record cannot be signed. Both weights, both times and a named primary nurse must be present, and the refusal names the missing fields.
  • The countersignature needs the nephrologist role, so a nurse cannot countersign as the nephrologist. The system does not compare the two names, so no account should hold both roles.
  • After locking, only the billing fields stay writable. The clinical record does not move again.
7Nephrologist
If needed

Amend, if something was wrong

A locked record still has one way in. The nephrologist amends it with a reason; the original values are preserved, the reason becomes a session note, and the before-and-after pair goes to the audit log. The record stays locked.

  • An amendment without a reason is refused. Otherwise it is indistinguishable from someone quietly fixing a number.
  • Amending is the only path in. There is no edit mode, no unlock, and no administrative back door.

After the treatment

Billing runs off signed records and nothing else. Two documents come out of a session: a claim, which is what the payer is asked for, and an invoice, which is whatever is left for the patient.

  1. Generate the claim from signed sessions. A session already on another claim stops the whole batch — a claim covering four of six sessions is worse than none, because someone then has to work out which two are missing.
  2. The rate is not in the software. It is read from the benefit programme in force on the service date. The PhilHealth case rate has moved ₱2,600 → ₱4,000 → ₱6,350 and the annual allotment 90 → 156; superseded programmes stay on file so an old claim re-prices against what applied at the time.
  3. Submit, then record the remittance exactly as the payer's advice states it. The claim's status follows the money — it cannot read "paid" while the figures say short-paid or denied. An approval of zero is a denial, and a denial needs a code or nobody can resubmit it.
  4. The invoice covers the balance, unless the programme forbids balance billing — in which case the patient's share is zero, whatever the list price came to.
Watch the allotment

You are told how many sessions of the annual allotment remain before you run out, not after. A patient discovering mid-year that their cover was exhausted last month is the failure this exists to prevent.

When the network drops

The bedside tablet is built to lose connectivity and carry on. This is the part of the system the unit depends on most, so it is worth knowing exactly what it promises.

  • Nothing you chart is lost. Every entry is written to the tablet first and sent when the network returns. The queue on the device is the authoritative copy; the patient list it shows you is only a cache.
  • Nothing is charted twice. Each entry carries an identifier minted on the device, so a batch that gets resent returns the stored answer without touching a clinical record again.
  • One bad entry cannot sink the rest. Each is processed on its own, so a nurse never loses four hours of charting to a single malformed row.
  • An expired login does not discard the queue. Unlock with your PIN and the same batch replays.
  • Conflicts are reported, not resolved silently. If a session locked while you were offline, your entry comes back to you as a conflict rather than being dropped or forced through.
  • Check-in, start and end wait for the network. They are where the water, chair, machine and dialyzer checks run, and a refusal is only worth anything before the needle goes in — so the tablet offers them only while it is connected.
The one thing to check

Before you put the tablet down at the end of a shift, confirm the outbox has drained. Charting that never left the device is still charting nobody else can see.

Stops you, or tells you

The distinction matters at six in the morning. A stop needs something physical done about it; a warning needs your judgement. Nothing in this system blurs the two.

What the system will not let go of

Some things are deliberately impossible. Knowing which saves you hunting for a setting that does not exist.

  • Chart views are logged — who opened whose record, and when. That is a different question from who changed what, and it is the one privacy regulators actually ask.
  • Nothing clinical is hard-deleted. Retiring a patient is a soft delete an administrator performs; the sessions, claims and audit trail all still point at the record.
  • Dry weight is effective-dated. Setting a new one does not rewrite last month's fluid figures, because each session recorded the target in force on its own day.
  • Patient status is a history, not a field. "When did this patient transfer out?" has to be answerable years later, and a single current-status column cannot answer it.
  • A patient has exactly one active prescription. Writing a new one closes the previous one on the same date; two overlapping prescriptions cannot exist.
  • Twice a day the system re-reads its own safety views looking for anything that slipped past — a cohort mismatch, an overdue dialyzer, a water exception — and reports what it finds; the Controls screen on the console shows the same list at any time. The report reaches a person only if the unit has wired it to one. A check nobody reads is not a check.

About this guide

Where each step happens. The water check, the daily board, the patient chart, signing, claims and invoices are on the console. Check-in, start, end and the flow sheet are on the bedside tablet. Medication doses and amendments have no screen yet: the system enforces the witness rule and the amendment rule described above, but neither can be exercised from either app today.

Clinical figures cited. Total chlorine 0.1 ppm (AAMI/ISO 23500); Kt/V ≥ 1.2 and URR ≥ 65% (KDOQI 2015); dialyzer TCV floor 80% of new; UF-rate concern threshold ~13 ml/kg/h. Benefit rates and session allotments are configuration, not code. Verify every one against your unit's standard operating procedures and the current payer circular before go-live.

Corrections in this issue. The text is the 20 August 2026 guide's. These statements were changed because the system behaves differently from how they described it:

  1. Who does what: the admin row said "user management and full access" — an administrator cannot chart, sign or prescribe, and grants roles but cannot create accounts in the app. The dietitian and readonly rows now say what those roles can actually reach.
  2. Step 0: "nothing else in the day can begin" became "no treatment can start" — the board and check-in do not wait for the water check; the first start does.
  3. Step 1: the board carries the cohort, not the prescription and dry weight (those attach at check-in), and add-ons and reschedules have no screen.
  4. Steps 2 and 3: the dialyzer is assigned and checked at the start, not at check-in, so that check moved to step 3.
  5. Step 4: medication doses have no screen yet.
  6. Step 5: a Kt/V sent from outside the tablet is replaced only when both BUN samples are recorded.
  7. Step 6: "one person cannot supply both signatures" — the system separates the signatures by role, not by person.
  8. Offline: added that check-in, start and end need the network.
  9. Stops you, or tells you: "annual allotment nearly spent" stops nothing; a claim larger than what remains does.
  10. Twice a day: the report "alerts a person" only if the unit has wired it to one, and the Controls screen shows the same list.
  11. About this guide: the note that there were "no screens yet" was replaced by where each step now happens.
